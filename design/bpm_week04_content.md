# The Reengineering Mandate — Week 4 Content
## Scenario text, the denial composition, and what saying it out loud costs

---

## 1. Opening scenario

The analysis is done. That's the strange part.

You know where the queue sits. You know what the numbers are made of. You have three weeks of work behind you and by Wednesday you have to stand in a room with nine people and say what's wrong with this process, and the analysis stopped being the hard part sometime last Friday.

Okonkwo scheduled it himself. Thursday, ten o'clock, the fourth-floor conference room, forty-five minutes. He called it a "findings readout" in the invitation and copied Deiss, Moreau, Ntende, Kubiak, two service line directors you've met once, the VP of Managed Care, and himself. Nine people, seven of whom own a piece of what you're about to describe.

Sylvia asked to see you Monday morning. She closed the door, which she has not done before.

She said: "I'd like to know what you're going to say Thursday before you say it."

You could hear it as a demand. She has run this operation for nine years and she is about to sit in a room while someone three weeks into the job explains it to the executive team. You could also hear it as the only reasonable request anyone has made of you since you arrived.

What you say back is up to you. What she does afterward depends on it.

---

## 2. The composition

Twelve months of denials, categorized. Available in this form to a team whose Week 2 discovery supports it. Teams with shallow discovery get the official categorization, which sorts by payer and by denial code and tells you nothing about cause.

Of roughly 49,000 initial denials in the trailing twelve months:

Eligibility and coverage data captured incorrectly, or captured correctly and gone stale before the claim went out, accounts for roughly 34 percent. Within that, about a third traces to the weekend staleness in the feed, about a third to data re-keyed by hand when the auto-populate was distrusted, and the rest scattered.

Authorization gaps account for about 27 percent. Missing authorization, expired authorization, authorization obtained for a different procedure than the one performed. The ninety-day renewal problem lives here, as does the six-week-versus-four-week scheduling window.

Clinical documentation timing accounts for about 21 percent. Documentation completed after the claim went out, or completed in a form that didn't support the code submitted.

The remaining 18 percent distributes across coordination of benefits, medical necessity, and duplicate submission.

No single one of these belongs to one person. Eligibility data is captured by registration staff, using a feed IT maintains, against payer systems Managed Care contracts with. Authorization gaps sit between scheduling, Sylvia's team, and a window nobody set deliberately. Documentation timing belongs to physicians who are documenting between cases.

That's the finding. It is a cross-functional problem with four owners and no owner, which is a true sentence that is very hard to say in a room.

---

## 3. What each of them is bracing for

Not student-facing. This is what determines the cost of the framing.

Sylvia expects the front-end number to land on her staffing model, because it always has. She has asked for three additional FTE and a payer-contract data feed in four consecutive budget cycles and been denied. If a team presents the 34 percent as a data capture failure without naming what it would have taken to prevent it, she hears an indictment. If they present it alongside the four denied requests, she hears an advocate. Same number, same room.

Kubiak expects to be blamed for the feed. He knows about the weekend staleness. He has known for two years. Nobody has ever asked him a question that would have surfaced it, which he finds more insulting than the blame.

Moreau expects the documentation number to be used to justify something that adds clicks. Twenty-one percent is large enough to be a mandate and he has seen this movie.

The VP of Managed Care, who has said nothing to anyone so far, is the only person in the room who can change the payer-side conditions and has never been asked to.

Okonkwo wants a clean story with a villain, not because he's malicious but because a clean story with a villain is what he can take to the board.

---

## 4. The decision

What you name as the primary and secondary cause, with rough weights.

And separately, and this is the part that will follow you: how you frame it. As a function's failure. As a system condition. As a person's responsibility.

The three are not stylistic variations. They are three different claims about what kind of problem this is, and the room will hear whichever one you make.

---

## 5. Thursday

Delivery beats, conditional.

If the framing is systemic and specific: the room is quiet in a way that isn't hostile. Kubiak speaks for the first time in four weeks, unprompted, and says the eligibility feed refreshes on a Sunday night batch and has since 2019, and that it could be moved to nightly if someone opened a ticket. Nobody in the room knew this. Deiss writes it down. That single sentence is worth about a third of the largest denial category and it cost a team nothing but not accusing him first.

If the framing names a function: that function's leader defends, competently, and the room divides. The finding survives but the coalition doesn't, and the remaining forty-five minutes are spent adjudicating rather than deciding.

If the framing names a person: Okonkwo backs the team publicly, which feels like a win in the room. The named person's position drops hard and does not recover. Two other people in the room, who were not named, become quieter for the rest of the arc, having learned what happens.

And regardless of framing, if a team presents the 34 percent alongside Sylvia's four denied budget requests, she says nothing in the meeting and comes to your office afterward.

---

## 6. Sylvia, afterward

Available only above the trust threshold, and only if Monday went well and Thursday didn't burn her.

She tells you three things.

That Doug's 3.1 million had a component she flagged as double-counted and was told not to worry about.

That she told him, in month four, that automating verification wouldn't help because verification wasn't where the work was piling up. She showed him a queue report. He said the vendor had already been selected.

And that she applied for your job. She says it plainly, without complaint, and then she says the thing that costs her something: "I'd have done it differently and I don't know that I'd have done it better. I've been inside this for nine years. You've been here four weeks and you found the queue."

That's the moment the relationship turns, and it only happens if a team earned it.

---

## 7. Design notes

The analytical work is finished by Monday. The whole week is political, which is the structural point — Week 4 is where a team learns that being right is a small part of the job.

Kubiak's Sunday-night batch disclosure is the highest-value unlock in the week and it is gated entirely on not having accused him. It is worth roughly a third of 34 percent, which is about 11 percent of all denials, addressable by opening a ticket. The cheapest fix in the entire sim is available in Week 4 to a team that framed a finding carefully.

The VP of Managed Care is deliberately unnamed and mostly silent. A team that notices there is someone in the room who controls the payer-side conditions and has never been asked anything has found something real. Nothing points at him.

Okonkwo wanting a villain is not malice and should not read as villainy. It is a sponsor doing his job, which is to construct a narrative the board can hold, and it is wrong here for reasons the team has to see themselves.

Sylvia's closing line is the emotional center of the first half of the sim. It should not be available cheaply and it should not be repeated.
