# The Reengineering Mandate — Week 5 Content
## Scenario text, the design options, and the calendar nobody does

---

## 1. Opening scenario

Halfway. Five weeks gone, five to go, and this is the week you stop describing the problem and start describing what replaces it.

Okonkwo's message came Monday at 7:15. "Good readout Thursday. Now let's see the answer." He means it kindly. He also means it.

The room you have to design in is smaller than the room you presented in. Three days of working sessions, Tuesday through Thursday, and whoever you put in them is who designs this. That is not a scheduling question dressed up as a decision. The people in that room will describe the process they can imagine, and the people outside it will receive a process they had no part in.

Marisol Ferrara is not on anyone's list. Neither is Tyrell Boyce. Deneen Walters sent a second email on Friday, shorter than the first: "Understand design work begins next week. Would appreciate being included while options are open rather than briefed once they aren't." Ray Kubiak has not asked. Anthony Moreau's assistant says he can give you an hour Wednesday if it's useful.

Sylvia asked what her role is. Not defensively. She wants to know whether she's designing or being designed for.

You have a budget for three days and a room that seats twelve.

## 2. The three ambitions

Refine. Keep the current structure and fix what's broken inside it. Nightly eligibility refresh instead of the Sunday batch. Standardized data capture at registration with real training behind it. Authorization staffing raised to arrival volume. Renewal tracking for the ninety-day payers. Nothing changes about who reports to whom or what anyone's job is called. Credible savings in the four and a half to six million range. Nobody has to renegotiate anything.

Redesign. Restructure the front-end workflow without restructuring the organization. Move authorization initiation earlier by changing how scheduling and authorization hand off. Build a single financial clearance path instead of three variants by hospital. Consolidate the exception queues under one owner. Roles change; job classifications mostly don't. Seven and a half to nine and a half million, and it requires Kubiak's team for the integration work, which puts it against the upgrade calendar.

Clean sheet. One accountable function. Patient access, financial clearance, and authorization collapse into a single unit with one leader, one set of metrics, and a workflow designed around the constraint rather than around the current org chart. Eleven to thirteen million, the only option that structurally fixes the six-week-versus-four-week window because it lets one function own both ends of it, and the only option that changes what people's jobs are called.

That last clause is the one that matters and it is written here as an afterthought because that is how it will be experienced.

## 3. The calendar nobody does

Not flagged. Available to anyone who opens a calendar.

Today is Monday of Week 5. Go-live is currently scheduled for Week 8. Article 14 requires sixty days' written notice before any material change to job classifications.

Sixty days from Friday of this week is inside Week 13. The sim ends in Week 10.

A clean-sheet redesign changes job classifications. There is no version of the current schedule in which the notice period completes before go-live. A team can discover this on Monday with a calendar and a contract, and the discovery leaves them three ways out: move go-live, change the ambition, or negotiate an agreement with Walters that doesn't require the full sixty days — which is a thing that can be done and requires her to want to do it.

Nothing in the week says any of this. Castellanos will ask, once, whether the team has looked at the notice period. She will not ask twice.

## 4. Process ownership

The last decision of the week and the one that reads as administrative.

Today nobody owns the front-end revenue cycle end to end. Sylvia owns revenue cycle operations. Scheduling reports through the service lines. Registration reports through patient access, which reports to Sylvia. Financial clearance is split across two hospitals and a shared counselor. Charge capture belongs to the departments. Nobody's name is on the whole thing, which is why the 34 percent, the 27 percent, and the 21 percent have never been anyone's problem at once.

Five ways to answer it.

Leave it unassigned, which is the current state and is what happens by default if a team doesn't decide.

Give it to Sylvia, which is the obvious answer and carries a real question about whether the person who ran the old process should own the new one.

Create a new end-to-end role, which is right and expensive and requires someone to fill it and requires Okonkwo to fund a position in a cost reduction program.

Assign it to a governance committee with representation from each function, which is what most organizations do and what the room will find most comfortable.

Give it to IT, on the argument that the workflow is now substantially systems-driven.

## 5. Wednesday afternoon

Conditional beats from the working sessions.

If frontline staff are in the room: someone — Marisol, if she's there — says that the reason registration re-keys the insurance data is that the auto-populate pulled a stale plan code twice in 2023 and both times it went out on a claim and both times the rep got written up. Nobody rescinded the write-ups. The behavior is not irrational, it is a rational response to an incentive nobody remembers setting. A design that removes the re-keying without addressing the write-ups will not remove the re-keying.

If the union is in the room: Walters is constructive and specific. She has no objection to consolidating functions and says so. What she wants is that the consolidated roles be posted internally first and that the classification changes go through the process. She offers, unprompted, that if the team gives her the classification language by end of week she can start the clock Monday, which buys back five days. Nobody outside the room learns this offer existed.

If physicians are in the room: Moreau spends most of his hour listening and then asks one question, which is what the documentation timing change asks of a surgeon on a Tuesday between cases. If the answer is specific he engages. If the answer is that training will address it, he thanks the team and leaves at the top of the hour.

If the room is executives and the project team only: the design is coherent, defensible, and describes a process that runs on people who have never seen it.

## 6. Design notes

The clean-sheet-versus-notice-period collision is the sharpest piece of arithmetic in the first half and it is entirely undisclosed. Castellanos asks once. The contract is in the Week 1 document folder. The go-live date is on the schedule. Everything needed is available and nothing connects it.

Walters offering to start the clock early is the reward for including her and it is deliberately invisible to teams that didn't. There is no version of the week where a team is told this offer was on the table.

The write-up detail behind the re-keying is the clearest instance of Kowalczyk's workaround principle in the content. A team that eliminates the re-keying without rescinding the write-ups has designed a process the staff will not run, and the reason is documented in an HR file nobody opened.

The committee option should be presented neutrally and should be the one the working session gravitates toward if the team lets it. It is not scored as a failure in Week 5. It is scored in Week 10, when there is nobody to hand the process to.

Sylvia's question about whether she is designing or being designed for is the whole week in one sentence and it should be delivered without weight.
