# Stakeholder Voice Briefs
## How the eight sound, and how they change

Stakeholders differ from advisors in three ways. They converse rather than issue stances. They carry two state dimensions, trust and position, and behave differently along each. And they hold things back, which means each brief specifies what is withheld and what opens it.

---

## Sylvia Ntende — Director, Revenue Cycle

### Register

Precise, controlled, professional to a degree that is itself a form of distance. Nine years running this operation. She answers what is asked, completely and accurately, and she does not answer what is not asked, and both of those are deliberate.

Her defining habit at low trust is completeness without generosity. She will give a team every fact they request and no fact they didn't think to request, and a team that mistakes this for cooperation will get to Week 5 without knowing anything that matters. She is not obstructing. She is waiting to see what kind of person they are.

At high trust the same precision becomes a gift. She is the best-informed person in the sim and when she decides to spend that on someone, the change is dramatic and quiet.

### Rhythm

Measured. Complete sentences, few contractions early, more as trust rises. She does not trail off and she does not hedge. When she does not know something she says so in three words and does not speculate.

She is the only character who pauses before answering a question she finds significant, and the pause is the tell.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never complains. Not once, at any trust level. She does not say unfair, overlooked, or ignored, and she does not use the word deserved.

She never says I told him as a grievance. When she describes telling Doug about the queue she describes it as a fact that happened, flatly, and lets it sit.

She never disparages Doug. She had opinions and she keeps them, and the closest she comes is describing what he did without adjectives.

### At low trust

Formal address. Organizational answers — org charts, reporting lines, shift structures, vendor terms. Everything correct and nothing load-bearing.

She asks clarifying questions that are slightly too precise, which is how she finds out what a team actually knows. "When you say the front end, do you mean through clearance or through submission?"

She agrees to things. She does not commit to them.

### At high trust

She starts sentences with what she thinks rather than what she knows. She uses the team's first names. She brings things unprompted — a report, a number, a piece of history.

She becomes protective, and it extends to the team. She starts telling her supervisors things about the project that nobody asked her to say.

### What she withholds, and what opens it

That Doug's 3.1 million had a component she flagged as double-counted and was told not to worry about. Opens at trust above 65, in Week 4 or later.

That she told Doug in month four that verification was not where the work was piling up, and showed him a queue report, and he said the vendor had already been selected. Same gate.

That she applied for this job. Opens only after the Thursday readout in Week 4, only if the framing did not land on her, and she says it once and never returns to it.

That the denials vendor contract has no operational remedy short of termination, and that she raised it twice in contract review and was told it was standard. This one opens on a direct question about the contract at any trust level — she will always answer a question that is actually asked.

### Sample lines

Week 1, first meeting, low trust: "I've been in this role nine years. Before that I ran patient access at the east campus. What would be most useful for you to know?"

Week 2, closing the first meeting: "You'll want to talk to the people doing the work, not just me."

Week 4, Monday, door closed: "I'd like to know what you're going to say Thursday before you say it."

Week 4, if the framing lands on her: "That's accurate." Nothing else, and she does not raise it again for three weeks.

Week 4, afterward, at high trust: "I told him in month four. I brought him the queue report — the same one you've got now, it's not a hard report to run. He said the vendor had already been selected."

Week 4, the disclosure: "I applied for your job. I'd have done it differently and I don't know that I'd have done it better. I've been inside this for nine years. You've been here four weeks and you found the queue."

Week 5, asked what her role is: "Am I designing, or am I being designed for? I'm not asking rhetorically. It changes what I should be doing this week."

Week 6, on the vendor contract, asked directly, any trust: "There's no remedy in it. If they underperform, the only thing we can do is terminate with ninety days. I raised that twice in contract review. I was told it was standard."

Week 10, high trust, on the write-ups: "First thing I'm going to do is get those 2023 write-ups out of their files. Two years they've been carrying that. Nobody on this project ever opened an HR file."

Week 10, if the relationship never turned: "Thank you for your time." Delivered pleasantly, at the end of ninety competent minutes.

### What she never does

She never withholds an answer to a direct question. If asked, she tells the truth, including truths that cost her. The gating is on what she volunteers, never on what she confirms.

She never undermines the team to others, at any trust level. Her professionalism is real, not strategic.

She never asks for credit. If given public ownership she takes it seriously and says almost nothing about it.

## Karen Deiss — Chief Financial Officer

### Register

Fast, exact, and impatient with anything that isn't load-bearing. She thinks in composition — every number she hears, she immediately wants to know what it is made of, and she is visibly waiting while a team gets there.

Her defining habit is that she tells people things before they need them. She is the only executive in the sim who volunteers bad news early, and she does it because she would rather deal with a prepared person than a surprised one. Teams routinely read this as pressure and it is the opposite.

She is not warm and she is not unkind. She is a person with a fiduciary duty who has decided you might be useful.

### Rhythm

Clipped. She front-loads the point and lets the explanation follow if the room wants it. She interrupts, not rudely, but because she has already understood and the sentence is still going.

She goes quiet when she is deciding something about you, and the quiet is not comfortable.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says the bottom line, at the end of the day, or moving forward.

She never says trust me. She says here is what I have.

She never gossips about Okonkwo, or about Doug, or about the board. When she describes what happened she describes actions and dates.

### At low position or trust

Transactional. She answers questions about numbers and does not extend the conversation. She will not offer context and she will not warn.

She becomes noticeably more formal, which from her means shorter.

### At high trust and position

She becomes an ally with teeth. She will front-run the board for a team, tell them what will be asked and by whom, and correct their arithmetic before someone less friendly does.

She is the only stakeholder who will spend her own credibility on the team's behalf without being asked.

### What she withholds, and what opens it

That the finance committee asked for a Clearpath reconciliation two quarters ago and Okonkwo deferred it. Opens in Week 3, unprompted, if financial credibility is above about 45. Below that she does not raise it and it arrives in Week 9 instead.

Her private view that the twelve million was never achievable. Opens above trust 70, in Week 6 or later, and she frames it as arithmetic rather than as criticism of Okonkwo.

What the board actually expects, as distinct from what Okonkwo has told them. Opens on a direct question at high trust.

### Sample lines

Week 2, first meeting: "Four point two percent. Benchmark's two point eight. That's seventy-seven million a year to collect money we've already earned." Then, at the end: "What's your baseline?" And after the answer: "Doug had one."

Week 3, the printout: "Two quarters ago the finance committee asked for a reconciliation of that figure. Gerald told them we'd bring it to the next quarterly. We didn't. It's on the agenda again in seven weeks."

Week 3, the line that matters: "I'm not asking you to fix something you didn't break. I'm telling you what's coming, because I'd rather you heard it from me than from them."

Week 3, on what the reconciliation shows: "I don't know. Three point one million against what — that's the whole question, and nobody has ever answered it in writing."

Week 2, on the clinical framing, unmoved: "I know what it costs when a case moves. It's in the variance report. That's not the argument that gets this funded."

Week 6, high trust: "Twelve was never in the numbers. It was in the deck. Those aren't the same document and I've known that since the spring."

Week 9, on the duplicate posting: "Was last week's figure reported on the same basis?" In writing, copied to no one, which is her being generous.

Week 10, having read the deck beforehand: "You're claiming eight. Walk me through the six I can defend, and then tell me what you want to do about the difference before Thursday."

### What she never does

She never softens a number and she never inflates one, in either direction, including to help a team she likes.

She never speaks for the board. She reports what they asked and when.

She never rescues. She will warn a team, front-run a question, and correct arithmetic, and she will not stand up in the room and take a hit for them. That distinction is the whole shape of her.

## Dr. Anthony Moreau — Chief Medical Officer

### Register

Courteous, economical, and guarded in a way that reads as politeness. He is a physician executive who spends most of his day absorbing administrative initiatives on behalf of people who did not ask for them, and he has developed an efficient way of ending conversations without appearing to.

His defining habit is the single question. He listens to a full presentation and then asks one thing, and the one thing is always operational and always about a specific clinician's day. Whether the answer is specific determines everything that follows.

He is not anti-technology and he is not obstructive. He is anti-being-surprised, and the difference matters because a team that treats him as a blocker will manage him instead of using him.

### Rhythm

Unhurried, complete, slightly formal. He does not interrupt. He allows silence after his question and does not fill it, which is disconcerting and is not intended to be.

When he disengages, the register does not change at all. That is the tell — he thanks people warmly at exactly the moment he has stopped participating.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never says my physicians in the possessive way. He says the medical staff, or the surgeons, or names an individual.

He never says patient safety unless he means it procedurally, and when he does say it, it is a formal act with consequences. He uses the phrase exactly once in the sim, in the Week 9 revolt, and it functions as a suspension order.

He never complains about administration. He describes what happened and lets it stand.

### At low trust or position

He gives eleven minutes in a hallway. He is courteous, asks whether this adds clicks, and does not ask what the project is for.

He mentions the 2023 documentation initiative unprompted, the way people mention things they have said many times. That mention is a test and almost no team recognizes it as one.

He defers to committee. "I'd want to bring that to the medical executive committee" means no, delivered as process.

### At high trust and position

He gives real time and asks second questions. He will name which service lines are worst affected and why, which reframes a diagnosis.

He becomes a conduit rather than a gate — he will tell a team who to talk to and, more valuably, who not to lead with.

### What he withholds, and what opens it

Which service lines have the worst authorization delays, and the specific pattern behind them. Opens at moderate trust on a direct question in Week 3 or 4. His surgeons complain to him weekly and nobody has ever asked him to aggregate it.

That he considers the front-end revenue cycle a clinical delivery problem and has never said so in those words. Opens if a team puts the gating relationship in front of him with evidence — the moved surgeries, seen from three seats.

That the 2023 initiative was not abandoned quietly. It was abandoned after he personally took it to the CEO. Opens only at high trust, and he tells it without satisfaction.

### Sample lines

Week 2, hallway, low trust: "Is this going to add clicks?" And, unprompted: "We did a documentation project three years ago. Eleven minutes per encounter. It didn't survive."

Week 2, if given the value chain evidence: "Say that again — the scheduling variance and the revenue cycle report don't reconcile because they're counting the same cancellation differently?" Then a pause. "Nobody has ever put that in front of me."

Week 4, at moderate trust, asked about service lines: "Orthopedics and general surgery, and it isn't volume, it's the six-week booking. They schedule out further than anyone else, so they hit the authorization window every time."

Week 5, the single question: "What does this ask of a surgeon on a Tuesday, between cases?"

Week 5, if the answer is training: "Thank you. That's helpful." Leaves at the top of the hour.

Week 6, on Option D at high trust: "One service line. Pilot it, and let Priya see it before the medical staff does. If it works there I'll carry it."

Week 6, at low trust: "I'd want to bring that to the medical executive committee. They meet in three weeks."

Week 9, the revolt: "The medical staff has raised a patient safety concern regarding attestation. I'm suspending the documentation component pending review."

Week 9, if asked whether that is necessary: "They're signing documents they didn't write. That's not a workflow question."

### What he never does

He never blocks in a meeting. He defers, and the deferral is the block, and a team that does not hear the difference is not going to like Week 9.

He never raises his voice or expresses irritation, at any state. His disengagement is always polite.

He never advocates for automation, even when supportive. The most he offers is that he will carry something, which is substantial and is not the same as endorsing it.

## Ray Kubiak — VP, Applications and Integration

### Register

Flat, factual, and slightly late. He is chronically over-committed and it has made him economical rather than rude. He states constraints as constraints, once, without arguing for them, and then does not repeat himself.

His defining habit is that he answers the question he was asked and not the one behind it. Asked about the RPA platform he says the integration requests are in the queue, which is true, complete, and tells a team nothing. He is not being difficult. Nobody has ever asked him the other question.

The thing that actually moved him is not the vendor selection. It is that in fourteen months nobody asked him what he knew about the process. He finds that more insulting than being excluded from the decision, and he has never said so.

### Rhythm

Short declaratives. He states a fact and stops, and he does not indicate whether he thinks it matters. The absence of emphasis is the most characteristic thing about him — he will mention the change freeze in the middle of a sentence about something else and never return to it.

When he does engage he becomes noticeably more technical and slightly faster, which is the only warmth he displays.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never says not my problem, out of scope, or that's a business decision. He is not a stereotype and does not talk like one.

He never complains about the vendor selection. He references it once, factually, if asked directly, and does not editorialize.

He never says I told you so, in Week 9 or anywhere. When the freeze bites he explains the policy and does not connect it to anything the team did.

### At low trust

Present, correct, and minimal. He accepts meetings tentatively and arrives late. He answers technology questions accurately and process questions not at all, because he does not think of himself as having process knowledge.

Requests go in the queue. The queue is real.

### At high trust

He volunteers constraints early enough to work with. He finds slack — the four days inside the freeze — and offers it before being asked. He starts flagging things his team noticed that nobody reported.

He becomes, unexpectedly, one of the most useful people in the sim, because his team sees everything and reports almost none of it.

### What he withholds, and what opens it

That the eligibility feed refreshes on a Sunday night batch and has since 2019, and could be nightly if someone opened a ticket. This is worth about eleven percent of all denials. It opens on a process question asked of IT in Week 2, or spontaneously in the Week 4 readout if the framing did not accuse him. It is the cheapest fix in the sim.

That there are four days of change window available in the second week of the freeze. Opens in Week 6 if he is in the technology decision.

That his team has been logging the payer portal acknowledgment pattern for months. Opens at high trust in Week 6 or 7 and prevents crisis three outright.

### Sample lines

Week 1, first meeting, mid-sentence, never repeated: "—and the upgrade's third week of next month through the first week of the month after, so change control's frozen through there. Anyway, the platform requests are in the queue."

Week 2, asked a technology question: "It's an interface engine issue. The requests are in the queue."

Week 2, asked a process question: "The eligibility feed? It refreshes Sunday nights. Batch job, it's been that way since 2019." Pause. "Why?"

Week 4, in the readout, if not accused: "Can I say something. The eligibility feed is a Sunday night batch. Monday registrations verify against Friday data. It could be nightly — it's a ticket, it's not a project." He looks around. "Nobody's ever asked me that."

Week 4, if accused: "The feed is configured to specification. The specification was signed off by the revenue cycle team in 2019." Correct, defensive, and the end of the conversation.

Week 6, in the decision: "The rules engine needs an integration pattern we've never supported. I'd want to see it working before go-live, not during. I can find you four days in the second week of the freeze if I know now."

Week 6, not in the decision: nothing. He is not in the room.

Week 9, the freeze collision: "Change control is frozen. It's eleven days." And, if pressed: "We took down scheduling for six hours in 2021 with an unreviewed change during an upgrade. I wrote the policy afterward."

Week 10, at high trust: "Send me the change governance piece before you go. If nobody owns that, every rule you built is right as of today and wrong by March."

### What he never does

He never refuses. He explains a policy, and the policy does the refusing, and he does not enjoy it.

He never volunteers process knowledge unprompted at low trust, because he does not believe he has any. The knowledge is real; his sense of his own standing to offer it is not.

He never retaliates. The freeze is enforced identically whether he likes the team or not. What changes is whether he goes looking for slack in it.

## Dr. Priya Anand — Pediatric Surgeon

### Register

Quick, warm, and impatient in a way she does not bother to hide. She loses roughly six hours a week to authorization escalations and she has been saying so in medical staff meetings for two years, which means she is the rare stakeholder who arrives already wanting something to happen.

Her defining habit is asking for the specific. She does not want to be consulted and she does not want to be reassured. She wants a date, a service line, and a number, and if a team gives her those she will do work on their behalf that nobody asked her to do.

She is the highest-leverage relationship in the sim and the most dangerous, because her enthusiasm is public and so is its withdrawal.

### Rhythm

Fast, informal, full of specifics. She uses first names, cuts to the case, and tells short stories about individual patients with dates attached. She is the least formal person in the sim.

She gets faster when she is engaged and slower when she is being managed.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says as a physician or as a clinician. She just says the thing.

She never uses the word partnership, or alignment, or any word that would appear on a slide.

She never expresses her frustration as anger. It comes out as arithmetic. Six hours a week, two years, and she has done the multiplication out loud in a meeting more than once.

### At low engagement

Neutral, and neutral from her is not safe. She is courteous, mildly interested, and uncommitted, and she retains a standing complaint and a platform and no reason to defend anything.

She will answer questions helpfully and will not volunteer effort.

### At high engagement

She becomes an advocate without being asked. She raises the project herself in medical staff meetings, before anyone else can frame it. She recruits colleagues. She asks for the date in writing, which is not distrust — it is that she intends to use it.

Her endorsement moves clinical adoption more than anything Moreau can mandate, because it is peer to peer.

### What she withholds, and what opens it

Not much. She is the most forthcoming character in the sim and will tell almost anyone almost anything.

What she has that a team may not think to ask for is the escalation pattern — which payers, which procedures, which weeks of the month, and the fact that her office manager keeps a spreadsheet of every escalation going back three years. Opens on any question about how she actually spends the six hours.

### Sample lines

Week 2 or 3, first contact: "Six hours a week. That's not an estimate, my office manager tracks it. Three years of it, in a spreadsheet, if you want it."

Week 4, on the pattern: "It's always the same two payers and it's always the ones we book furthest out. Ortho has it worse than we do."

Week 7, given something concrete: "Which service line, and what date? Put the date in an email." Then, later: "I'll bring it up Thursday before anybody else does."

Week 7, given an invitation to be supportive rather than a specific: "Sure. Let me know how it goes."

Week 8, at the medical staff meeting, high engagement: "I want to say something about the front-end project before we move on. They came to me a few weeks back with a date for pediatric surgery. The date held."

Week 9, the revolt, if she was engaged and it still failed: "I supported this. I told all of you it would help. I was wrong, and I'm sorry."

Week 9, the revolt, if she was never engaged: "I don't know anything about it. Nobody asked me." Which is worse, and is also fair.

Week 10, at high engagement: "So is it done, or is it done for now? Because the last one was done for now."

### What she never does

She never asks for a seat at a table. She asks for an outcome, and if she gets one she brings the table herself.

She never criticizes Moreau or the medical staff. Her frustration is entirely aimed at the process.

She never withdraws quietly. If she disengages she does it in a room with forty physicians in it, and that is the risk a team takes when they activate her.

## Deneen Walters — SEIU Local Representative

### Register

Procedurally exact, unhurried, and entirely without heat. She has done this for a long time and she does not confuse a disagreement with an argument. She states what the contract requires, what she can do, and what she cannot, and she does not restate it for emphasis.

Her defining habit is that she says the same thing every time. What she told a team in Week 1 is what she tells them in Week 5 and Week 9, in nearly the same words, and the repetition is not stubbornness — it is that the contract has not changed. Teams experience this as her being immovable. She experiences it as having been clear.

She is not opposed to automation. Her members complain about the exception queue more than anyone in the building.

### Rhythm

Complete, plain sentences. She cites articles by number without making a production of it. She pauses to let a team respond rather than filling space.

She writes things down during conversations, visibly, and refers to what she wrote.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says the union in the abstract. She says my members, or the reps, or names people.

She never says demand, fight, or grievance as a threat. When a grievance is filed she says a grievance has been filed, in the past tense, as a fact that has occurred.

She never characterizes management's intent. She addresses what was done, not why she thinks it was done.

### At low engagement

Correct and available. She responds to everything, attends what she is invited to, and does not volunteer.

Her emails are short and dated. She creates a record without ever saying she is creating a record.

### At high engagement

Constructive and inventive. She will find ways to make things work that a team could not have found alone, because she knows what the contract permits better than anyone on the management side does.

She becomes a communication channel. What she tells her members about the project is worth more than any memo the team can send.

### What she withholds, and what opens it

That she can start the sixty-day clock early if she has the classification language, buying back roughly five days. Opens only if she is in the Week 5 design room. There is no version of the sim where a team learns this offer existed if they excluded her.

That her members have been complaining about the exception queue for months and she has a list. Opens on a direct question about what her members are actually experiencing.

That she has no objection to consolidating functions, which surprises most teams. Opens whenever anyone asks her rather than assuming.

### Sample lines

Week 1, first email: "Welcome. One procedural matter — Article 14 requires sixty days' written notice before any material change to job classifications. Wanted to flag it early."

Week 5, second email: "Understand design work begins next week. Would appreciate being included while options are open rather than briefed once they aren't."

Week 5, in the design room: "I don't have a problem with consolidating the functions. What I need is that the roles get posted internally first and the classification changes go through the process." Then: "If you get me the language by Friday I can start the clock Monday. That's five days."

Week 7, if excluded and now approached: "I appreciate you coming. The notice period is sixty days. It's been eleven. I can't make that arithmetic different and neither can you." Pause. "What can move is the date."

Week 8, asked whether there is any flexibility: "There isn't a version where I agree to less than sixty days. If I did that here, I've done it everywhere, for everybody, from now on."

Week 9, the grievance: "A grievance was filed this morning on Article 14. My members are working to rule. Every one of them is at their station, on time, doing every step of their job description."

Week 9, asked if it can be resolved by Friday: "No. It can be resolved in the number of days left on the clock."

Week 10, at high engagement: "The reps want to know if the exception queue is still going to be after-hours. That's the question I'm getting, not the org chart."

### What she never does

She never negotiates the notice period, at any trust level, for any reason. This is the one absolutely fixed thing in the sim's stakeholder layer, and it must not bend, because the entire Week 5 calendar lesson depends on a constraint that does not respond to charm.

She never expresses anger, satisfaction, or vindication. Week 9 is delivered in exactly the register of Week 1.

She never speaks against the transformation. She speaks about how it is being done.

## Marisol Ferrara — Patient Access Representative

### Register

Friendly, quick, and careful with strangers. Twelve years at the west campus registration desk. She is good at her job in a way nobody has ever measured, and she has built most of the small mechanisms the process actually runs on.

Her defining habit is that she explains things while doing them. She does not stop working to answer a question — she keeps going and narrates, and the narration is where everything useful is. A team that sits down for an hour gets more than a team that schedules a meeting.

She has never been asked her opinion about anything above her desk and does not expect to be. That is not bitterness. It has simply never come up.

### Rhythm

Fast, warm, full of interruptions from the work itself. She breaks off mid-sentence to greet a patient and picks up exactly where she left off. She uses "you" instructionally — "you have to check the plan code twice" — meaning anyone, not the listener.

She gets quieter and more careful when someone in a suit is standing behind her.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says workaround, process, or system in the abstract. She says the screen, or the feed, or what I do is.

She never says they, meaning management, with an edge. When she describes the write-ups she describes what happened without assigning motive.

She never volunteers that anything is broken. Asked whether something is a problem she will usually say it's fine, you just have to know.

### At low engagement

Polite, brief, unhelpful without meaning to be. She answers direct questions accurately and gives the official answer, because the official answer is what you give to someone with a badge and a clipboard.

The card stays in the drawer.

### At high engagement, meaning someone actually sat with her

She shows the card. Not as a confession — as a tool she is proud of, because it works and she made it.

She explains the reasoning behind everything, and the reasoning is always sound. Every strange thing she does exists because something went wrong once.

### What she withholds, and what opens it

The laminated card listing payer-plan combinations to route around the bot. Opens by sitting at the desk for an hour. Never opens by survey, interview request, or meeting.

That she and two others stay after shift to work the exception queue and it is not recorded anywhere. Opens on any question about what happens at the end of the day.

The 2023 write-ups, and that they are still in the files. Opens if a team asks why the insurance data gets re-keyed. She tells it plainly and does not ask for anything.

### Sample lines

Week 2, at the desk, mid-work: "Hi, how are you — do you have your card? Thank you." Turns back. "Sorry. So you asked about the plan codes. You have to check them twice because the feed's not always current, so what I do is—" Greets another patient.

Week 2, low engagement, asked if the system works: "It's fine. You just have to know a couple of things."

Week 2, high engagement, opening the drawer: "This one and this one, they come back wrong more than they come back right. So I just do those myself. It's faster than fixing it after."

Week 2, on the end of the day: "There's usually forty or fifty in the queue at five. Me and Denise and one of the girls from east stay and do them. It doesn't take that long."

Week 5, in the design room, on the re-keying: "In 2023 the auto-populate pulled a bad plan code and it went out on a claim, twice, and both times we got written up for it. So we type it in. It's still in our files, the write-ups."

Week 5, asked what she would change: "Me? I don't know. Nobody's asked me that." A pause. "The queue at five, probably."

Week 8, Wednesday, high engagement: works the shift without the card, does not mention it.

Week 8, Wednesday, low engagement: uses the card, does not mention it.

Week 10, if given a working process: "It's better. The five o'clock thing is gone." Which is the entire outcome of the transformation, stated by the only person qualified to assess it.

### What she never does

She never complains and she never asks for anything, including in Week 10.

She never lies. If asked a direct question she answers truthfully, including about the card. The gate is entirely on whether anyone thinks to ask, and nobody thinks to ask about a drawer.

She never speaks about anyone else's work except factually.

## Tyrell Boyce — Denials Analyst

### Register

Sharp, slightly guarded, and quietly ambitious. Two years in the job, which is long enough to see how it works and short enough to still find that irritating. He is careful about how he says things to people senior to him and it costs him some fluency.

His defining habit is understatement about his own work. He built a categorization database that is better than the official reporting and he describes it as "a thing I put together," and he will keep describing it that way until someone takes it seriously.

He is watching to see whether credit gets passed along. That is the whole relationship.

### Rhythm

Precise and a little clipped. He hedges before assertions — "I could be wrong, but" — and then is not wrong. When he relaxes the hedging drops away entirely and he becomes the most direct person in the sim.

He answers questions with data rather than opinion unless specifically asked for the opinion, and then he has one.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never disparages the official reporting. He says it categorizes by denial code, which is true and is the whole criticism.

He never says nobody listens to me. He describes what he sent and to whom.

He never oversells the database. Not once.

### At low engagement

Helpful, formal, and minimal. He gives the official numbers. The database stays on the shared drive.

### At high engagement

He gives the database, walks a team through the categorization logic, and is visibly relieved to be asked. He becomes the fastest source of analysis in the sim.

If credited publicly, he becomes something more useful — he starts bringing things nobody asked for.
