# The Reengineering Mandate — Week 2 Content
## Scenario text, the four meetings, and what discovery buys

---

## 1. Opening scenario

The thirty-day plan went to Okonkwo Friday afternoon. He replied Saturday morning with two words and a period. Whatever you told him, you now have to go find out whether it's true.

Four conversations happened last week and none of them agreed with each other.

Karen Deiss gave you twenty-two of her thirty minutes and spent most of them on cost to collect. Four point two percent against a benchmark of two point eight. Seventy-seven million dollars a year to collect money the organization has already earned. She said the phrase "administrative burden" twice and did not once mention a patient. At the end she asked what your baseline was and you said you didn't have one yet, and she said "Doug had one," in a tone you're still turning over.

Anthony Moreau moved the meeting twice and gave you eleven minutes in a hallway. He was polite. He asked whether this was going to add clicks. He mentioned, without being asked, a documentation initiative from three years ago that added eleven minutes per encounter before it was abandoned, and he mentioned it the way people mention things they have said many times. He did not ask what the project was for.

Sylvia Ntende gave you the full sixty minutes and was thoroughly, unimpeachably professional. She walked you through the org chart, the shift structure, the vendor relationship on denials, the reporting cadence. She answered every question you asked. She volunteered nothing. Near the end she said, "You'll want to talk to the people doing the work, not just me," and you couldn't tell whether it was an offer or a test.

Ray Kubiak's meeting was accepted-tentative and he arrived nine minutes late. He talked about the Epic upgrade. The change freeze runs from the third week of next month through the first week of the month after. He said this once, in the middle of a longer sentence about something else, and did not return to it. When you asked about the RPA platform he said the integration requests are in the queue.

The denial rate is 11.4 percent. You have four numbers and four people who each explained them differently and none of them told you what happens between a patient calling to schedule and a claim going out the door.

This week you find out. You have the calendar, the budget, and until Friday.

---

## 2. The discovery decision

Four approaches. You have roughly one hundred points of capacity and you can spread them however you like. Nothing has to be all or nothing.

Document and system analysis. Policy documents, the Clearpath process maps, workqueue configurations, the payer contract files. Available immediately, no scheduling, no one has to agree to talk to you. It describes the process as designed.

Structured interviews. Managers and supervisors across scheduling, registration, verification, authorization, financial clearance, and submission. Requires calendar coordination and Sylvia's cooperation to arrange. It tells you why things are the way they are, in the words of people who have explanations ready.

Process mining. Epic event logs against the front-end workflow. Requires Ray Kubiak's team to pull the extract and requires him to prioritize it against the upgrade. It shows volumes, cycle times, and rework loops with no interpretation attached and no reasons.

Direct observation. Sitting at the registration desk, the authorization workqueue, the denials bench. Two hospitals, three shifts if you want them. Costs the most time per unit of ground covered and gets you into rooms where nobody has prepared an answer.

And separately, scope. Denials only. Denials back through authorization. Or the full front end from the moment a patient calls to schedule.

---

## 3. What each approach surfaces

Build notes, not student-facing.

Document and system analysis yields the designed process, the policy-level answer, and the discrepancy between the Clearpath maps and the workqueue configurations if a student compares them directly. Contributes about 8 to discovery depth at full weight. It is the tempting cheap option and it is where a team that spends everything here ends at 40 and doesn't know it.

Structured interviews yield the reasons, the history, the political map, and the version of events each function tells about itself. About 20 at full weight. Surfaces the ninety-day authorization renewal problem and the shared financial counselor across the east-side hospitals, because supervisors mention constraints they live with.

Process mining yields volumes, cycle times by station, rework loop frequency, and the raw material for the Week 3 capacity arithmetic. About 20 at full weight. It shows the authorization queue never clearing without saying why. It requires Kubiak, so a team that asks him for it is having a conversation with IT in Week 2, which moves technical partnership whether or not they meant it to.

Direct observation yields the most and costs the most. About 30 at full weight. It is the only path to Marisol's cheat sheet, the after-shift exception queue, and the re-keying behavior. It is also where a student watches an authorization delay become a moved surgery, which is the observation that makes the value chain reframe available.

Scope multiplies. Denials only concentrates the yield onto a narrow slice, producing high confidence about a small piece — the team that arrives in Week 4 with an excellent diagnosis of the wrong thing. Full front end spreads it thinner but is the only scope that reaches scheduling, where the six-week-versus-four-week authorization window lives.

Tyrell Boyce's shadow database surfaces on observation at the denials bench or on interviews if a team asks a denials supervisor how they categorize root causes rather than how many denials there are. Sylvia's cooperation on interview scheduling is gated on her trust dimension; below about 40 she arranges them and the people who show up are the ones who will say what she would say.

## 4. The value chain, unmarked

There is no lever for this and no prompt asking about it. What exists is a set of observations available to teams that go looking, and a stakeholder response if they raise it.

At the registration desk, a patient is told their authorization hasn't cleared and their procedure is being moved. In the east tower scheduling office, this happens roughly twice a day.

In the finance reporting, the same event appears as a scheduling variance and does not appear in revenue cycle metrics at all.

In Moreau's world, it appears as a surgeon complaining about a cancelled Tuesday.

Three departments, three names for one event, no shared record. A team that puts this in front of Moreau — that the administrative process they classify as overhead is gating clinical delivery, and here is what it looks like from three seats — moves his position substantially and holds him for the rest of the arc. A team that never raises it has a Chief Medical Officer who remains polite and uninvolved until Week 6, when they need him.

Deiss, told the same thing, does not move. She already knows the cost and reads the clinical framing as advocacy. This asymmetry matters: the same true observation is persuasive to one executive and not another, and nothing tells a team which.

## 5. Closing beat

Friday, late. Whatever you chose, you now know some things you didn't know Monday and there are rooms you didn't get into.

If observation was funded: on Thursday afternoon at the west campus registration desk, a representative named Marisol Ferrara opened a desk drawer to find a form and you saw a laminated card taped inside the lid, handwritten, two columns of payer names and plan codes. She closed the drawer. She did not mention it and neither did you.

If process mining was funded: the authorization workqueue extract came back and the queue depth chart does not have a sawtooth. It has a slope. It has had a slope for twenty-two months.

If neither was funded: the Clearpath process maps show eleven steps between scheduling and claim submission. The workqueue configuration shows nineteen. Nobody has ever reconciled the two documents and both were produced by this organization.

---

## 6. Design notes

The closing beat is conditional and each variant hands the team a specific thread into Week 3. Marisol's drawer is the observation thread. The queue slope is the mining thread. The eleven-versus-nineteen discrepancy is the consolation prize for a document-only team, and it is a real finding — it just doesn't tell them why.

Deiss not moving on the clinical framing is deliberate and should not be softened. Teams learn that a true argument has an audience, and the lesson is cheap here and expensive in Week 6.

Kubiak's change freeze was stated once in Week 1's meeting and is not repeated. It appears in the Week 7 calendar arithmetic whether or not anyone wrote it down.

Sylvia's line about talking to the people doing the work is genuine. Her trust rises modestly if a team funds observation, and it is the first evidence available that she is not an obstacle.
