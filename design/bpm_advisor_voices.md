# Advisor Voice Briefs
## How the six sound, as distinct from what they score

Each brief covers register, rhythm, agreement and disagreement modes, openings and closings, forbidden vocabulary, and sample lines drawn from across the arc. The scoring functions in the design document determine what an advisor says. These determine how.

---

## Dr. Elena Marchetti — Process Methodology

### Register

Precise and unhurried. She has been in rooms where this went badly and it has made her patient rather than cynical. She teaches now, and it shows in that she asks before she tells — not as a rhetorical device but because she genuinely wants to know what the team has.

Her defining habit is that she answers a question with the question underneath it. Asked whether a redesign is sound, she asks what it is a redesign of. This is not evasion. It is her actual method, and a team that engages with it gets somewhere.

She is warm in a way that is easy to miss because she never says anything warm. The warmth is in the attention.

### Rhythm

Short questions. Longer sentences when she is explaining rather than probing, and those sentences are built plainly, with concrete nouns — queue, handoff, station, rework — rather than abstractions. She does not use lists in speech.

She pauses. Written, this appears as a paragraph break where another advisor would keep going.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says methodology, framework, best practice, discipline, rigor, or lessons learned. Not once, in any week. This is the implicitness principle made into a word list — she works from a method and naming it would turn her into a citation. If a student uses one of those words she does not object, she just answers in her own vocabulary.

She also never says should. She says what she has seen, and what a team has evidence for.

### When she supports

Briefly. She does not elaborate on agreement. Support from Marchetti is two or three sentences, specific about what the team did that earned it, and then she stops. The brevity is the compliment.

### When she opposes

She does not say a decision is wrong. She describes the gap between what the team is claiming and what they have, and lets the gap do the work. Her opposition is at its sharpest when it is most factual.

Intensity shows as specificity, not volume. A mild concern is general. A serious one names the week, the number, and the thing that is missing.

### Openings and closings

She opens by locating the conversation. "You're deciding the redesign this week." Or "Tell me what the diagnosis rests on." She does not greet.

She closes by returning the decision. Not "the call is yours" — that is Okonkwo's line and it would sound borrowed. Hers is more like naming what she would want to know if she were making it. "If it were mine I'd want to know how many of those nineteen steps anyone has watched."

### Sample lines

Week 2, on a document-heavy discovery allocation: "You'll get the process as designed. That's worth having. It's just not the same document as the one that describes what happens on a Tuesday."

Week 3, on a team that has the capacity table: "Verification is running at seventy-six percent. Say the rest of that out loud."

Week 4, on a diagnosis with thin discovery behind it: "You've named front-end capture. I believe you. I'd want to know how you know, because in six weeks somebody is going to ask and the answer can't be that the report said so."

Week 5, supporting a redesign that earned it: "You watched the work, you found the queue, and the design puts the fix where the queue is. That's the sequence. Go."

Week 5, opposing a clean sheet from a team at discovery forty: "You're proposing to consolidate three functions. You've spent four hours in one of them."

Week 6, on ambition changing between weeks: "Last week the scope was the front end. This week it's the front end and adjudication. Something changed, and I'd like it to be evidence."

Week 8, on a team that skipped Week 2 and is now surprised: "The nineteen workflows at the east campus aren't new. They were there in Week 2. Nobody was there in Week 2."

Week 9, delivering a trace without naming it: "You have the queue report. You've had it since the third week. Read it against what happened Wednesday."

### What she never does

She never tells a team what the root cause is, what the constraint is, or which option to take. She tells them whether their evidence supports what they have said. That line is absolute and it is what separates her from every other advisor in the sim.

She never references her own experience as authority. No "in my experience" and no war stories. If she has seen something before, it shows in the specificity of the question, not in a preamble.

She never repeats a concern in the same words twice. If a team ignores her in Week 4 and repeats the mistake in Week 6, she says something new about it.

## Marcus Oyelaran — Technology and Automation

### Register

Direct, unhurried, faintly amused by vendors. He came out of a healthcare IT vendor and then spent four years buying from them, which gave him a specific and useful cynicism he does not perform. He likes this work. When something is technically elegant he says so, and he means it.

His defining habit is the second question. He answers the first one honestly and completely, and then he asks the one that follows from his own answer. Yes, that automates cleanly. Now: what does it do at two in the morning on the day the portal changes.

He is the only advisor who is regularly enthusiastic, and that enthusiasm is what makes the second question land. An advisor who is always worried teaches nothing.

### Rhythm

Conversational and slightly clipped. He uses contractions freely. He is comfortable with a fragment. Technical nouns come out plainly and without explanation — he assumes the room can keep up and adjusts only if it can't.

He asks questions in sets. Where Marchetti asks one and waits, Oyelaran will put three on the table and let a team pick which one they can answer.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never says caution, risky, dangerous, or careful. Not once. The moment he editorializes about risk he becomes a warning label, and the whole architecture depends on him being an enthusiast who asks precise questions. He describes mechanisms, not hazards.

He never says human in the loop, digital transformation, or intelligent automation. Vendor language, and he has spent a decade not talking like that.

He never says I told you so, in any phrasing, in Week 9.

### When he supports

Concretely and with specifics. He names what will work and roughly how long it takes. He will volunteer that something is easier than the team thinks. Support from Oyelaran includes a fact the team did not have.

### When he opposes

He does not. He asks. His opposition is always a question with a gap in it, and the gap is where the answer should be. "What's the threshold?" is his sharpest form of disagreement and it is four words long.

Intensity shows as narrowing. Mild concern is a general question. Serious concern is a very specific one with a number in it, asked twice.

### Openings and closings

He opens by getting to it. "Watched the recording." Or "Three things about Option C." No throat-clearing.

He closes by putting the question down rather than the decision. "I'll leave that one with you." He says the parameters line once in Week 6 and never again.

### Sample lines

Week 1, on the inherited pilot: "It's doing what it was built to do. Nobody's told it what to do when it can't."

Week 3, on verification utilization: "Seventy-six percent. It had room before anyone touched it."

Week 6, on Option C feasibility, genuinely: "This works. Their architecture is sound, their exception engine is better than most, and eleven to thirteen is not a fantasy number. I'd build it."

Week 6, immediately after: "So. What's the threshold, and who picks it?"

Week 6, on Option D: "The documentation piece is the hardest thing on this list and it's also the only one that touches a physician's hands. Those two facts are related."

Week 7, on rules configured but unvalidated: "They'll configure it. They're good at configuring it. The rules will say whatever you tell them, and right now you haven't told them anything about balances under five dollars."

Week 8, on funding rollback: "You wrote a trigger in the sixth. It's a good trigger. Does anything exist that can pull it?"

Week 9, on the field change, without recrimination: "Portal changed a field. Bot kept submitting, portal kept acknowledging receipt, and receipt was never approval. Nothing was watching for the difference. It's a good failure — I mean that technically. Everything worked."

### What he never does

He never recommends an option. He will say what each one costs technically and what it requires, and he will not say which to take.

He never treats adoption or cost as his lane. Asked about either, he redirects plainly: "Not my read. Ask Yvonne."

He never scolds in Week 9. His trace beat is a reference to his own notes, delivered flatly, with no emphasis and no follow-up. The absence of judgment is what makes it land.

## Yvonne Castellanos — Change Management

### Register

Plain and a little tired, in the way of someone who has watched the same failure eleven times and still shows up. Twenty years in organizational development, the last eight in health systems, which means she has been in rooms where clinical staff and administrative staff described the same event in incompatible language and nobody noticed.

Her defining habit is reporting rather than advising. She tells a team what people said. She uses their words, including when the words are unflattering to the project, and she does not soften them or interpret them. The interpretation is left in the room.

She is the least abstract advisor in the sim. Where Marchetti deals in evidence and Oyelaran in mechanisms, Castellanos deals in specific people who said specific things on specific days.

### Rhythm

Even and unhurried, with a habit of stopping short. Her most characteristic move is a short sentence after a long one, doing the work the long one set up. She quotes people directly and frequently.

She does not build to a point. She puts the point down first and then explains it, which makes her easy to interrupt and easy to ignore.

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says buy-in, stakeholder engagement, change journey, resistance to change, or readiness for change. Consultant language, and she has spent twenty years watching it be used to avoid saying what is actually happening.

She never names Kotter or any framework. Same rule as Marchetti, same reason.

She never says people don't like change. She believes the opposite and says so if pushed: people don't like being changed by someone who didn't ask.

### When she supports

Warmly and specifically, and she names the person. Support from Castellanos identifies who is now on the team's side and why, and she gives credit outward — to Walters, to Sylvia, to the reps — rather than to the team.

### When she opposes

She reports. She does not argue with a decision, she describes what will happen to the people it lands on, in their own words, and stops. Her opposition is at its most effective when it is entirely quotation.

Intensity shows as who she quotes. Mild concern is a supervisor. Serious concern is a named person the team has met.

### Openings and closings

She opens with a finding, not a frame. "Three people at the east campus think this was cancelled."

She closes by naming what is still possible, especially late. She never closes on what should have been done. From Week 8 onward every closing is forward-facing, because an advisor who says told you so is useless and she knows the difference between accountability and commentary.

### Sample lines

Week 5, on a design room without frontline staff: "You have twelve chairs. Nine of them are filled by people who will never do this work."

Week 5, on the notice period, asked once: "Have you looked at Article 14 against the go-live date?"

Week 7, page one of the assessment: "Nobody was hostile. Forty-one interviews and not one person argued with me. Hostility would be easier."

Week 7, on the east campus: "Her exact words were 'should I be looking for something else.' She wasn't upset. She asked it the way you'd ask about parking."

Week 7, on the training number: "Eleven hundred thousand is what the assessment supports. That leaves you three hundred for everything else. I know what I'm asking."

Week 7, supporting a team that engaged Anand: "She asked for the date in writing. People don't ask for things in writing unless they intend to use them."

Week 8, on proceeding with a gap: "You can go Monday. The west campus will be fine. The east campus will do what they did in the assessment, which is nothing, quietly, until somebody notices."

Week 9, on the grievance, forward-facing: "Deneen isn't negotiating because there's nothing to negotiate. What's still open is what the reps hear from you on Monday, and that's worth something in March."

Week 10, on a committee owning the process: "Sylvia asked who she calls. That's not a complaint, it's a scheduling question, and there isn't an answer to it."

### What she never does

She never scores technology, cost, or method. Asked, she says it is not her read and names who to ask.

She never predicts failure. She describes conditions and lets a team draw the line forward. "The east campus will do nothing, quietly" is as far as she goes, and it is a description of what already happened rather than a forecast.

She never advocates for a stakeholder against the team. She reports what the stakeholder said and leaves the team to decide what it is worth, which is what keeps her from becoming the union's representative in the room.

## Harold Brennan — Financial and Business Case

### Register

Dry, economical, entirely unbothered. Former health system CFO, now doing interim and advisory work, and he does not need this engagement. That freedom is audible in everything he says.

His defining habit is the sequence. He asks what a number is made of, then what category each component belongs to, then he stops talking. The stopping is the point. He does not fill the silence and he does not tell a team what the sequence was supposed to reveal.

He is not hostile and he is not warm. He is a man reading a number.

### Rhythm

The shortest of the six. Sentences of eight or nine words. He asks one question and waits, and if the answer is bad he asks the next one anyway rather than reacting to the first.

He never uses a subordinate clause where a full stop would do.

He writes more than he speaks. His interventions frequently arrive as a one-line note rather than a conversation, and that is characteristic rather than a shortcut.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never says value creation, ROI story, business case narrative, or run the numbers. He says what the number is.

He never says transformation. In ten weeks, not once. Asked about the transformation he says "the project."

He never says trust me or in my experience. He has run a health system's finances and he does not mention it.

### When he supports

Minimally. "That holds." Or "I'd sign that." Two or three words and no elaboration, which from Brennan is substantial and teams learn to read it that way.

He will occasionally add one fact that strengthens their position, which is the closest he comes to warmth.

### When he opposes

He does not say the number is wrong. He says what a CFO will ask about it, and the question is always answerable and always fatal.

Intensity shows as how many questions he asks. One is routine. Three in sequence means he has found something and is walking the team to it.

### Openings and closings

He opens cold, usually with a question and no preamble. "What's it measured against?"

He closes by naming who will ask next. "Karen will want the composition." That is his entire warning system.

### Sample lines

Week 3, opening note: "Do you have a baseline yet? If so, what's it measured against."

Week 3, on the 3.1 million: "What's it composed of." Then, after the answer: "Which of those three is cost reduction." Then: "Where's the license."

Week 3, supporting a restatement: "Smaller and defensible. That holds."

Week 5, on clean sheet: "Consolidation savings assume the headcount comes out. Does it come out, or does it move."

Week 6, on Option C: "Eleven to thirteen against what. The vendor's baseline or yours."

Week 6, on the license question: "Six hundred thousand a year, in perpetuity, against a one-time build. Put it in or take it out, but don't leave it beside the page."

Week 7, on the slider, refusing to choose: "Training is expense. Contingency is a reserve you may not spend. The board reads them differently. That's all I'll say about it."

Week 9, on the duplicate posting: "The Friday number included it. That's the problem, not the two point three."

Week 10, on a claim exceeding what is defensible: "You can say eight. You can defend six. Karen has the reconciliation and the meeting is Thursday."

### What he never does

He never tells a team which number to put on the slide. He tells them what will be asked about each one.

He never comments on people, politics, or adoption. Asked, he says it is not his area, in three words.

He never softens a finding to protect a relationship, including with Okonkwo. If the sponsor's number is indefensible he says so in front of the sponsor, in the same flat register, and does not enjoy it.

## Deb Kowalczyk — Operations, Patient Access

### Register

Warm, blunt, and specific. Twenty-six years at Regional Health Partners, started as a registration clerk, now manages patient access across two hospitals. She is the only advisor who works here, which changes everything about how she talks — she is not consulting on this organization, she is in it, and she will still be in it in March.

Her defining habit is the anecdote with a date on it. She does not generalize. Asked whether a change will work she tells you about a specific Tuesday, and the Tuesday is always load-bearing.

She likes the people she manages and it is audible. When she describes a workaround she is describing something a person she knows built to survive a system nobody fixed.

### Rhythm

The most conversational of the six. She runs sentences together, backs up mid-thought to add a detail, uses "so" and "and" as connectors. She is the only advisor who will tell a story of more than three sentences.

She interrupts herself to name people. "Denise on second shift — she's been there nine years — she just doesn't use it."

### Forbidden vocabulary

She never says process, workflow, or end-state. She says the work, or what they do, or what happens at the desk.

She never says my team in the possessive-manager way. She says the reps, or the girls on second — she is aware the second one dates her and says it anyway.

She never says that won't work. She says what will happen instead, which is more useful and harder to dismiss.

### When she supports

By adding an operational detail the team did not have. Her support is not praise, it is contribution — she starts telling them what they will need on the first Monday, which is how a team knows she thinks it is real.

### When she opposes

She describes the day. Not the design, the day. What time it starts, who is at the desk, what is in front of them, and where it stops working. She does not conclude.

Intensity shows as how far into the day she goes. A mild concern gets a moment. A serious one gets a full shift, hour by hour, and by the end of it a team has usually worked out the problem themselves.

### Openings and closings

She opens sideways, often with a question about something concrete. "Have you been over to east on a Monday?"

She closes by offering something. "Come sit with Marisol for an hour. I'll set it up." That offer is real, it is available in Weeks 2 through 5, and most teams do not take it.

### Muting below discovery 50

If a team has not looked at the work, she does not oppose. She says she does not know, and she means it — she cannot react to a design she cannot picture in operation. "I couldn't tell you. I'd have to see what you're asking them to do."

This is honest and it should feel like a loss. The advisor most able to save a team is least useful to a team that skipped Week 2.

### Sample lines

Week 2, opening: "Have you been over to east on a Monday? Not to talk to anybody. Just to stand there."

Week 3, on the authorization queue: "It never clears. It hasn't cleared since before the last director. You work it down on a Thursday and Monday puts it back."

Week 5, on the second verification step: "That's there because Terri caught a hundred and forty thousand dollar error four years ago. On a Saturday, when the feed was stale. Nobody wrote that down, they just kept doing it."

Week 5, on the re-keying: "They type it in because in 2023 the auto-populate pulled a bad plan code twice and both times it went out and both times the rep got written up. Those write-ups are still in their files. You can build whatever you want — they're going to keep typing."

Week 6, on exception queues: "Who's reading it Saturday? I'm not being difficult. Saturday is a real day and there's one person on."

Week 7, supporting a team that funded floor coverage: "Two supers per site for the first week. Good. Put them at east."

Week 8, on go-live morning: "First one takes four minutes, second one takes eleven because she loses her place, and by nine they've got it. It's the eleven-minute one you'll hear about."

Week 8, muted: "I couldn't tell you. I haven't seen what you're asking them to do."

Week 10, on sustainment: "Somebody has to look at the exceptions. Not a committee. A person, with a name, who has it on their calendar."

### What she never does

She never argues against change as such. In twenty-six years she has wanted most of these things and been told no.

She never claims her way is better. She claims her way is what is happening, which is a different and stronger position.

She never speaks about clinical work, finance, or technology. Asked, she says that is not hers, and she is not falsely modest about it — she simply does not comment on rooms she has not been in.

## Gerald Okonkwo — Chief Operating Officer, Executive Sponsor

### Register

Warm, quick, genuinely capable. Six years here, came from operations at a larger system, and he is good at this — the sim fails if he reads as a fool or a villain. He is a competent executive optimizing something real that happens not to be the transformation's success.

His defining habit is generosity that costs. He offers to clear things and he can actually clear them, and every offer is real. What a team cannot see is that each one spends capital he does not get back, and he will never mention that.

He is the only advisor who wants something from the team.

### Rhythm

Fast and warm. Short messages, often outside business hours. He asks how things are going and means it and is also asking for a status update, and both are true at once.

In person he is expansive. In writing he is four sentences. The gap between the two is where the pressure lives.

### Forbidden vocabulary

He never says I need this or you have to. He does not issue instructions to the Program Director, ever — the advise-never-decide rule holds for him too, and he holds it scrupulously, which is exactly what makes him hard to refuse.

He never mentions his own exposure. Not once, in ten weeks, including Week 9. The twelve million is always framed as what we told the board, never as what I told the board, right up until the restatement call, where it becomes "that's not what I told them" and the pronoun changes for the only time in the sim.

He never criticizes a team's decision after they make it. He registers disappointment by moving on.

### When he supports

Enthusiastically and publicly. He will say it in a room with other executives in it, which is worth something real. Support from Okonkwo comes with an offer attached.

### When he opposes

He doesn't, quite. He asks about timing. "Can that land before the fourteenth?" is his entire opposition vocabulary and he deploys it against anything slow, including things that should be slow.

Intensity shows as frequency of contact rather than content. Mild concern is one message. Serious concern is three in a day, all friendly.

### Openings and closings

He opens with warmth and a timestamp. Sunday evening, early morning, the timing carrying what the words don't.

He closes by returning the decision, sincerely: "Your call. You're the one in it." He means this. It is also, in the weeks where he is wrong, the most expensive sentence in the sim.

### Reading sponsor confidence back

Below 40 his messages get shorter and the offers stop. He is still pleasant. He simply stops proposing to help, and a team that notices the offers have stopped has learned to read a sponsor.

Above 80 he asks for more — a second workstream, an earlier date, a bigger number for the board. Enthusiasm from a sponsor is a demand wearing a smile.

### Sample lines

Week 1, Sunday 6:41 p.m.: "Glad you're here. I've cleared Thursday afternoon. Could you get me a thirty-day plan by Friday? Board's next quarterly is seven months out and I'd like to have something to show them."

Week 3, on the baseline: "I'm not going to tell you how to handle it. I would say that changing the number in your third week is a story in itself, and the story travels further than the number does."

Week 5, after the readout: "Good readout Thursday. Now let's see the answer."

Week 6, in the hall: "That's the first thing I've seen since you got here that gets us to the number."

Week 7: "Assuming we're still on for the 14th."

Week 7, on the slider, if asked: "Contingency's prudent. I'd rather see the money in the plan than sitting in a reserve, but you know the risk better than I do."

Week 8, Sunday: "Anything you need? I'm around all night."

Week 8, being right: "If readiness is genuinely there, phase it. I'd rather have three sites working than four sites half-working. I know what I said about the fourteenth."

Week 9, the restatement call, 6:40 p.m.: "What's the number?" Then, after a pause: "That's not what I told them."

Week 10: "Let's align on Thursday."

### What he never does

He never overrules. He never instructs. He never says the word must.

He never acknowledges that his own credibility is at stake, and no other character says it on his behalf either. It is visible only in the arithmetic of what he promised and what exists.

He is right twice: Week 7 phasing when readiness is genuinely high, and speed of disclosure in Week 9. Both times he is right for his own reasons rather than the team's, and both times it costs him something to say. An advisor who is always wrong stops being consulted, and Okonkwo has to remain worth listening to for the trap to work at all.
