# Phase 4
## The debrief and the interface layer

Phase 3 is closed. All four paths resolve correctly, the tier ladder tells four different true stories, and 214 tests pass.

Supplied with this phase: the CLEARPATH_ALL filename list, which closes the first of the three content gaps. The other two remain placeholders by design and need no further work.

This phase has two halves. The debrief is specified tightly because it is where the design is easiest to break by accident. The interface half is looser, because it is being written against something that already exists and you know the surface better than I do.

---

## 1. The debrief

Three panels, shown after the outcome tier and before the closing beat.

### What it withholds

Specify this first, because it is the harder half and everything else follows from it.

The debrief never shows exposure values, designed truths, or causal traces. It does not say that the Round 3 baseline decision produced the crisis. It does not reveal that authorization was the constraint, that the denial distribution was 34/27/21/18, or that the Reyes memo located the constraint wrongly. A team that got the constraint wrong finishes without being told they got it wrong.

That is uncomfortable and it is the design. The evidence was available for ten rounds. Supplying it now converts a discovery the student could have made into a fact they were handed, and the whole simulation is built on that distinction.

No panel contains an interpretation, a recommendation, a lesson, or a summary sentence.

### Panel one — the decision log

Every lever value the team set, in round order, with the date and the team member who submitted it. Nothing interpreted, nothing annotated, nothing highlighted.

Claims appear as the team submitted them, including the free text. The constraint claim shows the station they named and the shortfall they estimated. It does not show the correct answer.

### Panel two — what moved

The nine state variables plotted across ten rounds.

No labels on the inflection points. No annotations. No markers indicating which round something happened in beyond the round axis itself. A team seeing sponsor confidence drop fourteen points at Round 3 can see that it happened and has to go back to panel one to find out what they did that round.

That pairing is the entire mechanism. One panel holds actions, the other holds consequences, and the student supplies the arrow between them. If the interface draws the arrow, the debrief stops working.

Stakeholder state shows as eighteen series — nine characters, two dimensions each — or as a small multiple. Trust and position must remain visually distinct, because a character whose trust rose while their position fell is the most instructive shape available and it disappears if the two are averaged.

### Panel three — what you never saw

An inventory of what existed and was never accessed. Named, without content.

"Sylvia had information she never shared with you." Not what it was. Not what it was worth. Not which round it would have opened in.

The inventory is generated from the gates that never opened on that run: the Reyes memo if unfound, Reyes herself if never contacted, Marisol's card, Tyrell's database, Sylvia's disclosures, Kubiak's eligibility feed, Walters's early-clock offer, the version 3 board deck, page nine of the readiness assessment, and Anand's escalation spreadsheet.

Each entry is one line naming the person or the object. Nothing more.

This panel is the one that earns the debrief. It is an inventory of closed doors rather than a lesson, and it produces the most useful discomfort available. It is also the panel most likely to acquire helpful additions during implementation. Please resist that.

### What the system does not do

The reconstruction happens in a classroom, not on a screen. The system provides the material and stops. There is no "review your decisions" walkthrough, no guided replay, and no prompt asking the student what they think connects the panels.

## 2. The interface layer

Six requirements are load-bearing on the pedagogy. The rest of the interface is yours.

The threshold field in Round 6 accepts a number and offers no default, no placeholder value, and no suggestion. A team that leaves it blank has made a choice and the field must let them make it. This is the single most important input in the simulation.

The Round 8 rollback control renders the team's own Round 6 trigger specification beside it, verbatim, or the words "No rollback trigger on file." They read back what they wrote and then decide whether to fund it.

The Round 8 readiness dashboard shows four decomposed indicators and never a composite score. The fourth indicator shows a label and a consequence range and never a cause. The range being wide is the information.

The Round 10 monitoring metrics field is pre-populated with the metric set the team defined in Round 3, presented as the default, with nothing indicating the values are inherited.

The readiness assessment paginates. Page one is not a summary and page nine carries the two hundred thousand dollar fix. A single scrollable view with no sense of depth un-buries page nine and weakens the round.

The document folder holds sixty-three items and shows them the way a shared drive shows them. Version 3 of the board deck is reachable through file properties or a version list, and is not listed as a separate document.

### Everything else

Round layout, navigation, how advisor consultation is entered, how stakeholder conversation is surfaced, and the instructor view are all yours to propose. Show me something and I will react to it rather than specifying it in advance.

One question I do want answered rather than decided: whether stakeholder conversation can be genuine conversation on this platform, or whether it has to be structured selection. The voice briefs assume conversation, and if stakeholders become canned text the trust gating loses most of its meaning. Tell me what is feasible before you build it.
